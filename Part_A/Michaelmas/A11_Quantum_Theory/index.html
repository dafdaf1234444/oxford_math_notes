0. Classical particles and waves

0.1 Point particles

A point particle has position \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) obeying Newton's second law. Conservative force \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla V\). Total energy \(E = \frac{1}{2}m|\dot{\mathbf{x}}|^2 + V\).

\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\dot{\mathbf{x}}|^2 + V(\mathbf{x}) \] (0.2)
\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = m\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}\cdot\frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{dt^2} + \nabla V \cdot \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\!\left(m\frac{d^2\mathbf{x}}{dt^2} - \mathbf{F}\right) = 0 \]
(0.3)
\[ E_{\text{kinetic}} = \frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{2m} \]
(0.4)
\[ m\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{F} = -\nabla V \] (0.1)

0.2 Waves

The classical wave equation admits plane wave solutions. These solutions will motivate the quantum mechanical wave function.

\[ \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial t^2} = \nabla^2 \phi \] (0.5)

Plane wave solutions take the form:

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x},t) = A\,e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)} \] (0.6)
\[ v = \frac{\omega}{|\mathbf{k}|} \]
(0.7)

1. Physical background and wave-particle duality

The photoelectric effect and atomic spectra show classical physics fails at atomic scales. Wave-particle duality emerges from these experiments, motivating a new theoretical framework.

1.1 The photoelectric effect

When light shines on a metal surface, electrons may be emitted. Classical wave theory predicts that any frequency of light should eventually liberate electrons if the intensity is high enough. Experiment contradicts this: there is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of intensity.

Definition 1.1.

Light of angular frequency \(\omega\) exists in quanta (photons) of energy \[E = \hbar\omega.\] The photoelectric effect gives \(E_{\text{kinetic}} = -E_0 + \hbar\omega\), where \(E_0\) is the binding energy. No electrons are emitted when \(\omega < E_0/\hbar\).

\[ E = \hbar\omega \] (1.1)
Remark 1.1.

The energy of a photon \(E = \hbar\omega = h\nu\) where \(\nu = \omega/2\pi\) is the frequency. For visible light \(\nu \sim 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}\) gives \(E \sim 1\,\text{eV}\). This is characteristic of energy scales in atomic physics. The relation also applies to relativistic particles via \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\), though this course treats only non-relativistic quantum mechanics.

1.2 Emission and absorption spectra

Atoms emit and absorb light only at discrete frequencies, producing line spectra. This is evidence for discrete energy levels within atoms.

\[ \omega_{n_1,n_2} = 2\pi R_0 c\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad n_2 > n_1 \geq 1 \] (1.2)
\[ E_n = -\frac{2\pi R_0 \hbar c}{n^2} \]
(1.3)

This is the Rydberg formula, with \(R_0\) the Rydberg constant. It describes the frequencies of all spectral lines of atomic hydrogen.

1.3 Double slit experiment

When particles pass through two slits, they produce an interference pattern on a screen, just like waves. This holds even when particles are sent one at a time, demonstrating the wave nature of matter.

1.4 De Broglie matter-waves

De Broglie extended Einstein's photon relation to all particles, associating a wavelength with any particle of momentum \(\mathbf{p}\).

Definition 1.2.

A free particle of energy \(E\) and momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) is associated with a wave of angular frequency \(\omega\) and wave vector \(\mathbf{k}\) via the de Broglie relations: \[E = \hbar\omega, \qquad \mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}.\] The wavelength is \(\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/|\mathbf{p}| = h/|\mathbf{p}|\).

\[ \mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k} \] (1.4)

2. Wave mechanics

The de Broglie relations lead to the Schrodinger equation governing quantum matter-waves.

2.1 The Schrodinger equation

Combining the de Broglie relations with the classical energy-momentum relation \(E = p^2/2m + V\) and replacing \(E \to i\hbar\partial_t\), \(\mathbf{p} \to -i\hbar\nabla\) acting on a wave function \(\Psi\), we obtain the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics.

Definition 2.1.

A single non-relativistic particle of mass \(m\) in potential \(V(\mathbf{x})\) is governed by the Schrodinger equation: \[i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi + V\Psi.\] This is a linear complex PDE for the wave function \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)\). Cf. Definition 1.2.

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hbar\omega\,\Psi = E\,\Psi, \qquad -i\hbar\nabla\Psi = \hbar\mathbf{k}\,\Psi = \mathbf{p}\,\Psi \]
(2.2)

For a free particle (\(V = 0\)), this reduces to:

\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x}, t) = A \exp\!\bigl[i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)\bigr] \]
(2.1)
\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi \] (2.5)
\[ E = \frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{2m} + V \]
(2.6)

The general Schrodinger equation with potential is the central equation of wave mechanics:

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi + V(\mathbf{x})\Psi \] (2.7)
\[ \frac{i\hbar\dfrac{dT}{dt}}{T} = \frac{-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi}{\psi} \]
(2.8)
\[ i\hbar\frac{dT}{dt} = E\,T \]
(2.9)
\[ T(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar} \]
(2.10)
\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \sum_n c_n\,\Psi_n(\mathbf{x},t) \] (2.13)
\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} + V\,\Psi \]
(2.14)

Superposition principle: the Schrödinger equation is linear, so any linear combination of solutions is again a solution. This is a key structural feature.

2.2 Stationary states

We look for solutions of the form \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), separating the time and space dependence.

Definition 2.2.

Writing \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), the time-independent Schrodinger equation is: \[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi.\] Solutions are stationary states with definite energy \(E\). Cf. Definition 2.1.

\[ \omega = \frac{E}{\hbar} = \frac{\hbar|\mathbf{k}|^2}{2m} \]
(2.3)
\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} \] (2.11)
\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi \] (2.12)

2.3 One-dimensional equations

In one dimension, the time-independent Schrödinger equation becomes an ODE:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi \] (2.15)
\[ \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} \]
(2.16)
\[ V(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & 0 < x < a, \\ +\infty, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
(2.17)
\[ \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\,\psi \]
(2.18)
\[ \psi(x) = \begin{cases} A\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\,x\right) + B\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\,x\right), & E > 0, \\[6pt] A + Bx, & E = 0, \\[6pt] A\cosh\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}\,x\right) + B\sinh\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}\,x\right), & E < 0. \end{cases} \]
(2.19)
\[ 0 = \psi(a) = \begin{cases} B\sin\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\,a\right), & E > 0, \\[6pt] Ba, & E = 0, \\[6pt] B\sinh\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}\,a\right), & E < 0. \end{cases} \]
(2.20)
\[ \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar} = \frac{n\pi}{a} \]
(2.21)
\[ \psi_n(x) = \begin{cases} B\sin\!\dfrac{n\pi x}{a}, & 0 < x < a, \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]
(2.22)

For bound states in one dimension we require \(\psi(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\), plus continuity of \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) where \(V\) is continuous. At infinite potential walls, \(\psi\) must vanish.

2.4 Particle in a box

Consider a particle confined to the interval \([0,a]\) with infinite potential walls. The boundary conditions \(\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0\) force quantized energies.

Definition 2.3.

When energies are discrete and bounded below, the lowest energy is the ground state (zero-point) energy. Higher energies are the first, second, etc. excited states. For example, a particle in a box \([0,a]\) has energies \(E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\) for \(n = 1,2,3,\ldots\).

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = E\,\Psi = \frac{|\mathbf{p}|^2}{2m}\,\Psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi \]
(2.4)
\[ \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \] (2.23)
\[ E_n \simeq 5.97 \times 10^{-18}\,n^2 \;\text{J} \]
(2.24)
\[ E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \] (2.25)
\[ V(x,y,z) = \begin{cases} 0, & 0 < x < a,\; 0 < y < b,\; 0 < z < c, \\ +\infty, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
(2.26)
\[ \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\,\psi \]
(2.27)
\[ \psi_{n_1,n_2,n_3}(x,y,z) = B\sin\frac{n_1\pi x}{a}\sin\frac{n_2\pi y}{b}\sin\frac{n_3\pi z}{c} \]
(2.28)

2.5 Particle in a 3D box

For a particle in a three-dimensional rectangular box \([0,a_1] \times [0,a_2] \times [0,a_3]\), the Schrödinger equation separates into three independent one-dimensional equations. The energies are sums of the individual contributions:

\[ E_{n_1,n_2,n_3} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n_1^2}{a_1^2} + \frac{n_2^2}{a_2^2} + \frac{n_3^2}{a_3^2}\right), \qquad n_1,n_2,n_3 \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \] (2.29)
\[ \psi = \sum_{i=1}^{d} c_i\,\psi_i \]
(2.30)
\[ E_{n_1,n_2,n_3} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\bigl(n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2\bigr) \]
(2.31)

2.6 Degeneracy

Definition 2.4.

An energy level \(E\) is \(d\)-fold degenerate if the space of stationary states with that energy has dimension \(d > 1\). Non-degenerate means \(d = 1\). For example, a particle in a 3D box has degeneracies due to different combinations of quantum numbers giving the same energy.

\[ d(E) = \#\{(n_1,n_2,n_3) \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}^3 : E_{n_1,n_2,n_3} = E\} \] (2.32)
\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\,\psi \]
(2.33)
\[ \psi(x) = \psi(x + 2\pi R) \]
(2.34)
\[ \psi(0) = \psi(2\pi R), \qquad \psi'(0) = \psi'(2\pi R) \]
(2.35)
\[ \psi_{\text{g.s.}} = A, \qquad E_{\text{g.s.}} = 0 \]
(2.36)

2.7 Particle on a circle

A particle constrained to move on a circle of radius \(R\) has periodic boundary conditions \(\psi(\theta + 2\pi) = \psi(\theta)\). This quantizes the angular momentum to integer multiples of \(\hbar\).

\[ \psi_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{in\theta}, \quad E_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{2mR^2}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \] (2.37)

Each energy level with \(n \neq 0\) is 2-fold degenerate (corresponding to \(\pm n\)). The angular momentum in this system is \(L = n\hbar\).

Remark 2.1.

Throughout this course we work with \(L^2\) spaces. The inner product on \(L^2([0,a])\) is \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \int_0^a \bar\psi(x)\phi(x)\,dx\). The stationary states of the particle in a box form a complete orthonormal set: any square-integrable function on \([0,a]\) can be expanded as a Fourier sine series. This is a concrete instance of the spectral theorem (Theorem 5.3).

3. The Born interpretation

The Born interpretation gives physical meaning to the wave function in terms of probability.

3.1 Probability density

Definition 3.1.

The probability density is \(\rho(\mathbf{x},t) = |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\). The probability of finding the particle in a region \(D\) at time \(t\) is: \[P_\Psi(D) = \iiint_D |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3x.\]

\[ P_\Psi(D) = \iiint_D |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} \]
(3.2)
\[ \rho(\mathbf{x},t) = |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2 \] (3.1)
Example 3.1.

For the particle in a box with \(\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/a}\sin(n\pi x/a)\), the probability density is \(\rho_n(x) = (2/a)\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\). The probability of finding the particle in the left half \([0,a/2]\) is exactly \(1/2\) for every \(n\), by symmetry. See Definition 3.1, Equation (2.13).

Example 3.2.

The expectation value of position for the \(n\)-th stationary state of a particle in a box is \(E_{\psi_n}(x) = \int_0^a x\,|\psi_n(x)|^2\,dx = a/2\) for all \(n\). The particle is equally likely to be found on either side of the centre, by symmetry. See Definition 3.3.

\[ 0 < \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} < \infty, \qquad \forall\; t \]
(3.3)
Example 3.3.

Consider \(\psi(x) = Ax(a-x)\) on \([0,a]\) and zero outside. Normalization: \(\int_0^a A^2 x^2(a-x)^2\,dx = A^2 \cdot a^5/30\), so \(A = \sqrt{30/a^5}\). Expanding in box eigenstates: \(c_n = \langle\psi_n|\psi\rangle = 4\sqrt{15}/(n\pi)^3\) for odd \(n\), and \(c_n = 0\) for even \(n\) (by parity). The probability of measuring the ground state energy is \(|c_1|^2 = 960/\pi^6 \approx 0.9986\). See Definition 3.4.

Definition 3.2.

A wave function \(\Psi\) is normalizable if \(0 < \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi|^2\,d^3x < \infty\). It is normalized if \(\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi|^2\,d^3x = 1\). The Born interpretation requires normalization. Cf. Definition 3.1.

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3x = 1 \] (3.4)
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = 1 \]
(3.5)
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi_n(x,t)|^2\,dx = |B|^2 \int_0^a \sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}a\,|B|^2 \]
(3.6)
\[ \Psi_n(x,t) = \begin{cases} \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{a}}\,\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{a}\;e^{-in^2\pi^2\hbar t/2ma^2}, & 0 < x < a, \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
(3.7)
\[ \rho(\mathbf{x},t) = |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2 = \bigl|\psi(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}\bigr|^2 = |\psi(\mathbf{x})|^2 \]
(3.8)
\[ \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\psi(\mathbf{x})|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} = 1 \]
(3.9)
\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = A\exp\!\left[i\!\left(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \frac{\hbar|\mathbf{k}|^2}{2m}t\right)\right] \]
(3.10)
\[ \rho_n(x) = \frac{2}{a}\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{a} = \frac{1}{a}\left(1 - \cos\frac{2n\pi x}{a}\right) \]
(3.11)
\[ F_n(x) = \int_0^x \rho_n(y)\,dy = \frac{x}{a} - \frac{1}{2n\pi}\sin\frac{2n\pi x}{a} \]
(3.12)
\[ \int_{a/4}^{3a/4} |\psi_n(x)|^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi}{2} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}, & n \text{ even}, \\[4pt] \frac{1}{2} + \frac{(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{n\pi}, & n \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]
(3.13)
\[ E_\Psi(f(\mathbf{x})) = \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} f(\mathbf{x})\,|\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} \]
(3.14)
\[ E_\Psi(f(x)) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx \]
(3.15)
\[ E_{\Psi_n}(x) = \int_0^a x\,\rho_n(x)\,dx = \frac{a}{2} \]
(3.16)
\[ \langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn} \] (3.26)
\[ c_m = \int_0^a f(x)\,\psi_m(x)\,dx \]
(3.27)

Orthonormality of stationary states. This, combined with completeness, allows Fourier-type expansion of any state in the energy eigenbasis.

\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad c_n = \langle\psi_n|\Psi(\cdot,0)\rangle \] (3.28)
\[ \sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1 \] (3.29)
\[ \Psi(x,0) = \begin{cases} A\,x(a - x), & 0 < x < a, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
(3.30)
\[ \int_0^a |\Psi(x,0)|^2\,dx = |A|^2 \int_0^a x^2(a-x)^2\,dx = |A|^2\,\frac{a^5}{30} \]
(3.31)
\[ c_n = \frac{4\sqrt{15}}{n^3\pi^3}\bigl[1 - (-1)^n\bigr] = \begin{cases} 0, & n \text{ even}, \\[4pt] \dfrac{8\sqrt{15}}{n^3\pi^3}, & n \text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]
(3.32)
\[ \Psi(x,t) = \sqrt{\frac{30}{a}}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\!3} \sum_{\substack{n=1,3,5,\ldots}} \frac{1}{n^3}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\;e^{-in^2\pi^2\hbar t/2ma^2} \]
(3.33)
\[ P_\Psi(\text{ground state}) = |c_1|^2 = \left(\frac{8\sqrt{15}}{\pi^3}\right)^{\!2} \simeq 0.9986 \]
(3.34)

Parseval's identity: the total probability of obtaining some energy on measurement is 1. This is the completeness relation expressed in terms of expansion coefficients.

3.2 Continuity equation

Definition 3.3.

The expectation value of a function of position \(f(\mathbf{x})\) in state \(\Psi\) is: \[E_\Psi(f(\mathbf{x})) = \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} f(\mathbf{x})|\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3x.\] This gives the average value of repeated position-dependent measurements. Cf. Definition 3.1.

Proposition 3.1.

The Schrodinger equation implies the continuity equation: \[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0,\] where \(\mathbf{j} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\Psi\nabla\bar{\Psi} - \bar{\Psi}\nabla\Psi)\) is the probability current.

Proof.

Direct computation: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2 = \bar{\Psi}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + \Psi\frac{\partial\bar{\Psi}}{\partial t}\). Substituting the Schrodinger equation and its conjugate, the potential terms cancel (since \(V\) is real), leaving \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}\).

□
\[ \mathbf{j} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(\Psi\nabla\bar{\Psi} - \bar{\Psi}\nabla\Psi\bigr) \] (3.18)
\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2 = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(\bar{\Psi}\,\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\,\nabla^2\bar{\Psi}\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla\cdot\bigl(\bar{\Psi}\,\nabla\Psi - \Psi\,\nabla\bar{\Psi}\bigr) = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} \]
(3.19)
\[ \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} \]
(3.20)
\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0 \] (3.17)
Proposition 3.2.

If the probability current \(\mathbf{j}\) decays sufficiently fast at infinity, then \(\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3}|\Psi|^2\,d^3x\) is independent of \(t\). In particular, if \(\Psi\) is normalized at some time \(t_0\), it remains normalized for all \(t\). Cf. Proposition 3.1, Definition 3.2.

Proof.

Integrate the continuity equation over \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and apply the divergence theorem. The surface integral vanishes by the decay condition on \(\mathbf{j}\).

□

3.3 Measurement of energy

Definition 3.4.

For a state \(\Psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), the probability of measuring energy \(E_n\) is \(|c_n|^2\). This follows from orthonormality of stationary states. Cf. Definition 2.2.

\[ P(E_n) = |c_n|^2, \qquad \sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1 \] (3.21)
\[ \mathbf{j}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{j}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,r^2\,dS_{\text{unit}} \]
(3.22)
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3\mathbf{x} = 0 \]
(3.23)
\[ \frac{d}{dt}P_\Psi(D) = -\iint_S \mathbf{j}\cdot d\mathbf{S} \]
(3.24)
\[ f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n\,\psi_n(x) \]
(3.25)

4. The harmonic oscillator

Near any potential minimum, physics is described by a harmonic oscillator. This section solves it via the Schrodinger equation.

4.1 One-dimensional harmonic oscillator

Definition 4.1.

A 1D stationary state with \(\psi(-x) = \psi(x)\) has even parity; with \(\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)\) it has odd parity. For symmetric potentials \(V(-x) = V(x)\), stationary states can always be chosen to have definite parity.

\[ V(x) = V(0) + \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2, \]
(4.2)

5. The mathematical structure of quantum theory

The postulates of quantum mechanics are formalized using Hilbert spaces and self-adjoint operators.

5.1 States

Definition 5.1.

The states of a quantum system are elements of a complex inner product space \(\mathcal{H}\). Proportional vectors \(\psi\) and \(\lambda\psi\) (for \(\lambda \neq 0\)) represent the same physical state.

\[ \left|\int_a^b \overline{\varphi(x)}\,\psi(x)\, dx\right|^2 \leq \int_a^b |\varphi(x)|^2\, dx \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\, dx, \]
(5.2)
Definition 5.2.

A Hilbert space is a complete complex inner product space. Completeness ensures that infinite sums (superpositions) converge to elements of \(\mathcal{H}\). The inner product is denoted \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\), antilinear in the first argument and linear in the second.

\[ \|\psi\|^2 \equiv \langle \psi | \psi \rangle, \]
(5.3)
\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \overline{\langle\psi|\phi\rangle}, \quad \langle\phi|\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2\rangle = \alpha\langle\phi|\psi_1\rangle + \beta\langle\phi|\psi_2\rangle, \quad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle \geq 0 \] (5.1)

5.2 Observables

Definition 5.3.

Observables (measurable quantities) correspond to self-adjoint (Hermitian) linear operators \(A\) on \(\mathcal{H}\), satisfying \(\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|\psi\rangle\) for all states \(\phi, \psi \in \mathcal{H}\). Ref: Definition 5.2.

\[ \text{(i)}\;\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle = \overline{\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle}, \quad \text{(ii)}\;\langle\varphi|\alpha_1\psi_1 + \alpha_2\psi_2\rangle = \alpha_1\langle\varphi|\psi_1\rangle + \alpha_2\langle\varphi|\psi_2\rangle, \quad \text{(iii)}\;\langle\beta_1\varphi_1 + \beta_2\varphi_2|\psi\rangle = \bar{\beta}_1\langle\varphi_1|\psi\rangle + \bar{\beta}_2\langle\varphi_2|\psi\rangle, \quad \text{(iv)}\;\|\psi\|^2 \geq 0. \]
(5.4)
Definition 5.4.

Position: \((X_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = x_i\psi(\mathbf{x})\). Momentum: \((P_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = -i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_i}\). Both are self-adjoint on suitable domains. Ref: Definition 5.3.

\[ \|\psi\|^2 = 0 \;\text{ if and only if }\; \psi = 0. \]
(5.5)
\[ \langle\phi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|\psi\rangle \quad \text{(self-adjointness)} \] (5.7)

\[ X\psi = x\psi, \qquad P\psi = -i\hbar\psi', \]

8. Angular momentum

In classical mechanics, angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{x} \wedge \mathbf{p}\). Conservation of angular momentum plays a key role in the hydrogen atom.

8.1 Angular momentum operators

Definition 8.1.

The angular momentum operator \(\mathbf{L} = (L_1, L_2, L_3)\) has components: \[L_i = \sum_{j,k} \epsilon_{ijk} X_j P_k,\] where \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is the Levi-Civita alternating symbol. See Definition 5.4.

\[\epsilon_{ijk} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } ijk \text{ is an even permutation of } 123, \\ -1 & \text{if } ijk \text{ is an odd permutation of } 123, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\]
(8.2)
Proposition 8.1.

The angular momentum components satisfy the following commutation relations:

(i) \([L_i, X_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} X_k\).

(ii) \([L_i, P_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} P_k\).

(iii) \([L_i, L_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} L_k\).

The last relation shows that angular momentum components do not commute. See Definition 8.1, Proposition 6.3, Proposition 6.4.

Proof.

For (i): \([L_i, X_j] = \sum_{m,n}\epsilon_{imn}[X_mP_n, X_j] = \sum_{m,n}\epsilon_{imn}X_m[P_n,X_j] = -i\hbar\sum_m \epsilon_{imj}X_m = i\hbar\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}X_k\). Parts (ii) and (iii) are similar, using the Leibniz rule and the canonical commutation relations.

□
\[L_1 = X_2 P_3 - X_3 P_2,\quad L_2 = X_3 P_1 - X_1 P_3,\quad L_3 = X_1 P_2 - X_2 P_1.\]
(8.1)
\[ [L_i, L_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk}\,L_k \] (8.4)
\[[L_i, X_j] = \sum_{m,n=1}^{3} \epsilon_{imn}[X_m P_n, X_j] = -i\hbar \sum_{m=1}^{3} \epsilon_{imj}\, X_m = i\hbar \sum_{m=1}^{3} \epsilon_{ijm}\, X_m.\]
(8.5)
Corollary 8.3.

\(L_i\) commutes with \(|\mathbf{X}|^2 = X_1^2+X_2^2+X_3^2\), with \(|\mathbf{P}|^2\), and with \(\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{P}\). See Proposition 8.1, Definition 5.5.

Proof.

Quantum mechanics has applications beyond physics. The BB84 protocol, developed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984, uses the quantum measurement postulate and the no-cloning theorem to establish secure communication channels. Any eavesdropping attempt disturbs the quantum states being transmitted, making interception detectable. This is a direct consequence of the collapse of the wave function: measuring a quantum state inevitably changes it.

The security of quantum key distribution rests on fundamental quantum mechanical principles rather than computational assumptions, unlike classical cryptographic methods such as RSA. The uncertainty principle ensures that an eavesdropper cannot simultaneously determine all properties of the transmitted quantum states.

11. Epilogue

The formalism developed in this course provides the foundation for more advanced quantum mechanics. Possible directions include:

Relativistic quantum mechanics: replacing the Schrödinger equation with the Dirac equation to accommodate special relativity, leading to the prediction of antimatter.

Quantum field theory: promoting fields to operators, enabling the description of particle creation and annihilation.

Quantum information: exploiting entanglement and superposition for computation and communication beyond classical limits.

Many-body quantum mechanics: extending the single-particle formalism to systems of identical particles, with applications to condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry.

A. Fuchs' theorem

This appendix states the existence theorem for power series solutions of ODEs with regular singular points, used in the analysis of the hydrogen atom radial equation.

Theorem A.1.

Consider the ODE \(y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = 0\). If \(x = x_0\) is a regular singular point (i.e., \((x-x_0)p(x)\) and \((x-x_0)^2q(x)\) are analytic at \(x_0\)), then there exists at least one solution of the form: \[y(x) = (x-x_0)^\sigma \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n(x-x_0)^n,\] where \(\sigma\) is a root of the indicial equation and the series converges in a neighbourhood of \(x_0\). This is a Frobenius series.

B. Representations of angular momentum

This appendix develops the representation theory of angular momentum, proving that the quantum number \(j\) takes values in \(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\).

Proposition B.1.

Let \(\mathbf{J}\) be an angular momentum operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\). Then \(\mathcal{H}\) decomposes as a direct sum of invariant subspaces \(V_j\), each of dimension \(2j+1\), on which \(J^2\) acts as \(j(j+1)\hbar^2\) and \(J_3\) has eigenvalues \(m\hbar\) for \(m \in \{-j, -j+1, \ldots, j\}\). Ref: Theorem 8.6.

Theorem B.2.

For each \(j \in \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\), there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) irreducible representation of the angular momentum algebra of dimension \(2j+1\). The matrix elements of \(J_\pm\) in the standard basis \(\{\ket{j,m}\}\) are: \[J_\pm\ket{j,m} = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m\pm 1)}\,\ket{j,m\pm 1}.\] Refs: Proposition B.1, Proposition 8.5.

Corollary B.3.

Integer values \(j = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) correspond to orbital angular momentum representations (realised by spherical harmonics). Half-integer values \(j = \frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}, \ldots\) correspond to spin representations (requiring internal degrees of freedom). Refs: Theorem B.2, Definition 8.4.

C. Angular momentum in spherical polars

In spherical polar coordinates \((r, \theta, \phi)\), the angular momentum operators take the following forms:

\[L_3 = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},\]

\[L_\pm = \hbar e^{\pm i\phi}\left(\pm\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + i\cot\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right),\]

\[L^2 = -\hbar^2\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right).\]

The eigenvalue equations \(L^2 Y_l^m = l(l+1)\hbar^2 Y_l^m\) and \(L_3 Y_l^m = m\hbar Y_l^m\) then reduce to ODEs in \(\theta\) and \(\phi\), whose solutions are the spherical harmonics. Ref: Definition 8.4.
