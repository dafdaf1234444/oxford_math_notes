Quantum Theory
Mark Mezei, Michaelmas Term 2024

0. Classical particles and waves

0.1 Point particles

A point particle has position \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) obeying Newton's second law. Conservative force \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla V\). Total energy \(E = \frac{1}{2}m|\dot{\mathbf{x}}|^2 + V\).

\[ m\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{F} = -\nabla V \] (0.1)

0.2 Waves

The classical wave equation admits plane wave solutions. These solutions will motivate the quantum mechanical wave function.

\[ \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial t^2} = \nabla^2 \phi \] (0.3)

Plane wave solutions take the form:

\[ \phi(\mathbf{x},t) = A\,e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x} - \omega t)} \] (0.5)

1. Physical background and wave-particle duality

The photoelectric effect and atomic spectra show classical physics fails at atomic scales. Wave-particle duality emerges from these experiments, motivating a new theoretical framework.

1.1 The photoelectric effect

When light shines on a metal surface, electrons may be emitted. Classical wave theory predicts that any frequency of light should eventually liberate electrons if the intensity is high enough. Experiment contradicts this: there is a threshold frequency below which no electrons are emitted, regardless of intensity.

Definition 1.1.

Light of angular frequency \(\omega\) exists in quanta (photons) of energy \[E = \hbar\omega.\] The photoelectric effect gives \(E_{\text{kinetic}} = -E_0 + \hbar\omega\), where \(E_0\) is the binding energy. No electrons are emitted when \(\omega < E_0/\hbar\).

\[ E = \hbar\omega \] (1.1)

1.2 Emission and absorption spectra

Atoms emit and absorb light only at discrete frequencies, producing line spectra. This is evidence for discrete energy levels within atoms.

1.3 Double slit experiment

When particles pass through two slits, they produce an interference pattern on a screen, just like waves. This holds even when particles are sent one at a time, demonstrating the wave nature of matter.

1.4 De Broglie matter-waves

De Broglie extended Einstein's photon relation to all particles, associating a wavelength with any particle of momentum \(\mathbf{p}\).

Definition 1.2.

A free particle of energy \(E\) and momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) is associated with a wave of angular frequency \(\omega\) and wave vector \(\mathbf{k}\) via the de Broglie relations: \[E = \hbar\omega, \qquad \mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}.\] The wavelength is \(\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/|\mathbf{p}| = h/|\mathbf{p}|\).

\[ \mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k} \] (1.2)

2. Wave mechanics

The de Broglie relations lead to the Schrodinger equation governing quantum matter-waves.

2.1 The Schrodinger equation

Combining the de Broglie relations with the classical energy-momentum relation \(E = p^2/2m + V\) and replacing \(E \to i\hbar\partial_t\), \(\mathbf{p} \to -i\hbar\nabla\) acting on a wave function \(\Psi\), we obtain the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics.

Definition 2.1.

A single non-relativistic particle of mass \(m\) in potential \(V(\mathbf{x})\) is governed by the Schrodinger equation: \[i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi + V\Psi.\] This is a linear complex PDE for the wave function \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)\). Cf. Definition 1.2.

For a free particle (\(V = 0\)), this reduces to:

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi \] (2.1)

The general Schrodinger equation with potential is the central equation of wave mechanics:

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi + V(\mathbf{x})\Psi \] (2.4)

2.2 Stationary states

We look for solutions of the form \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), separating the time and space dependence.

Definition 2.2.

Writing \(\Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), the time-independent Schrodinger equation is: \[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi.\] Solutions are stationary states with definite energy \(E\). Cf. Definition 2.1.

\[ \Psi(\mathbf{x},t) = \psi(\mathbf{x})\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} \] (2.7)
\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi \] (2.8)

2.3 One-dimensional equations

In one dimension, the time-independent Schrodinger equation becomes an ODE, which can be solved explicitly for simple potentials.

2.4 Particle in a box

Consider a particle confined to the interval \([0,a]\) with infinite potential walls. The boundary conditions \(\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0\) force quantized energies.

Definition 2.3.

When energies are discrete and bounded below, the lowest energy is the ground state (zero-point) energy. Higher energies are the first, second, etc. excited states. For example, a particle in a box \([0,a]\) has energies \(E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\) for \(n = 1,2,3,\ldots\).

\[ E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \] (2.15)

2.5 Particle in a 3D box

For a particle in a three-dimensional rectangular box \([0,a_1] \times [0,a_2] \times [0,a_3]\), the Schrodinger equation separates into three independent one-dimensional equations. The energies are sums of the individual contributions.

2.6 Degeneracy

Definition 2.4.

An energy level \(E\) is \(d\)-fold degenerate if the space of stationary states with that energy has dimension \(d > 1\). Non-degenerate means \(d = 1\). For example, a particle in a 3D box has degeneracies due to different combinations of quantum numbers giving the same energy.

\[ d(E) = \#\{(n_1,n_2,n_3) \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}^3 : E_{n_1,n_2,n_3} = E\} \] (2.22)

2.7 Particle on a circle

A particle constrained to move on a circle of radius \(R\) has periodic boundary conditions \(\psi(\theta + 2\pi) = \psi(\theta)\). This quantizes the angular momentum to integer multiples of \(\hbar\).

3. The Born interpretation

The Born interpretation gives physical meaning to the wave function in terms of probability.

3.1 Probability density

Definition 3.1.

The probability density is \(\rho(\mathbf{x},t) = |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\). The probability of finding the particle in a region \(D\) at time \(t\) is: \[P_\Psi(D) = \iiint_D |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3x.\]

\[ \rho(\mathbf{x},t) = |\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2 \] (3.1)
Definition 3.2.

A wave function \(\Psi\) is normalizable if \(0 < \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi|^2\,d^3x < \infty\). It is normalized if \(\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi|^2\,d^3x = 1\). The Born interpretation requires normalization. Cf. Definition 3.1.

3.2 Continuity equation

Definition 3.3.

The expectation value of a function of position \(f(\mathbf{x})\) in state \(\Psi\) is: \[E_\Psi(f(\mathbf{x})) = \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} f(\mathbf{x})|\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^2\,d^3x.\] This gives the average value of repeated position-dependent measurements. Cf. Definition 3.1.

Proposition 3.1.

The Schrodinger equation implies the continuity equation: \[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0,\] where \(\mathbf{j} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\Psi\nabla\bar{\Psi} - \bar{\Psi}\nabla\Psi)\) is the probability current.

Proof.

Direct computation: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2 = \bar{\Psi}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + \Psi\frac{\partial\bar{\Psi}}{\partial t}\). Substituting the Schrodinger equation and its conjugate, the potential terms cancel (since \(V\) is real), leaving \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}\).

□
\[ \mathbf{j} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(\Psi\nabla\bar{\Psi} - \bar{\Psi}\nabla\Psi\bigr) \] (3.6)
\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j} = 0 \] (3.7)
Proposition 3.2.

If the probability current \(\mathbf{j}\) decays sufficiently fast at infinity, then \(\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3}|\Psi|^2\,d^3x\) is independent of \(t\). In particular, if \(\Psi\) is normalized at some time \(t_0\), it remains normalized for all \(t\). Cf. Proposition 3.1, Definition 3.2.

Proof.

Integrate the continuity equation over \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and apply the divergence theorem. The surface integral vanishes by the decay condition on \(\mathbf{j}\).

□

3.3 Measurement of energy

Definition 3.4.

For a state \(\Psi = \sum_n c_n \psi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), the probability of measuring energy \(E_n\) is \(|c_n|^2\). This follows from orthonormality of stationary states. Cf. Definition 2.2.

\[ P(E_n) = |c_n|^2, \qquad \sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1 \] (3.21)

4. The harmonic oscillator

Near any potential minimum, physics is described by a harmonic oscillator. This section solves it via the Schrodinger equation.

4.1 One-dimensional harmonic oscillator

Definition 4.1.

A 1D stationary state with \(\psi(-x) = \psi(x)\) has even parity; with \(\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)\) it has odd parity. For symmetric potentials \(V(-x) = V(x)\), stationary states can always be chosen to have definite parity.

\[ V(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 \] (4.4)

The Hermite differential equation arises after substituting the ansatz \(\psi(\xi) = h(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}\) into the time-independent Schrodinger equation for the harmonic oscillator:

\[ h''(\xi) - 2\xi\,h'(\xi) + 2n\,h(\xi) = 0 \] (4.14)
Theorem 4.1.

The 1D quantum harmonic oscillator with potential \(V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2\) has energies: \[E_n = \left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.\] The stationary state wave functions are \(\psi_n(x) \propto H_n(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}\) where \(\xi = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x\) and \(H_n\) are the Hermite polynomials. The ground state energy is \(\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega\) (zero-point energy). Cf. Definition 2.2, Definition 2.3.

\[ E_n = \left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \] (4.18)

4.2 Higher-dimensional oscillators

In higher dimensions, the harmonic oscillator potential separates into a sum of independent one-dimensional oscillators. The total energy is the sum of individual energies. Quantum tunnelling into classically forbidden regions is a characteristic quantum phenomenon.

5. The mathematical structure of quantum theory

The postulates of quantum mechanics are formalized using Hilbert spaces and self-adjoint operators.

5.1 States

Definition 5.1.

The states of a quantum system are elements of a complex inner product space \(\mathcal{H}\). Proportional vectors \(\psi\) and \(\lambda\psi\) (for \(\lambda \neq 0\)) represent the same physical state.

Definition 5.2.

A Hilbert space is a complete complex inner product space. Completeness ensures that infinite sums (superpositions) converge to elements of \(\mathcal{H}\). The inner product is denoted \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\), antilinear in the first argument and linear in the second.

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \overline{\langle\psi|\phi\rangle}, \quad \langle\phi|\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2\rangle = \alpha\langle\phi|\psi_1\rangle + \beta\langle\phi|\psi_2\rangle, \quad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle \geq 0 \] (5.1)

5.2 Observables

Definition 5.3.

Observables (measurable quantities) correspond to self-adjoint (Hermitian) linear operators \(A\) on \(\mathcal{H}\), satisfying \(\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|\psi\rangle\) for all states \(\phi, \psi \in \mathcal{H}\). Ref: Definition 5.2.

Definition 5.4.

Position: \((X_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = x_i\psi(\mathbf{x})\). Momentum: \((P_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = -i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_i}\). Both are self-adjoint on suitable domains. Ref: Definition 5.3.

\[ (X_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = x_i\,\psi(\mathbf{x}) \] (5.8)
\[ (P_i\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = -i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_i} \] (5.9)
Lemma 5.1.

If \(A, B\) are self-adjoint operators on \(\mathcal{H}\), then so are \(A + B\), \(\alpha A\) (for real \(\alpha\)), and \(A^2\). This allows construction of observables like the Hamiltonian from position and momentum. Ref: Definition 5.3.

Proof.

For \(A+B\): \(\langle\phi|(A+B)\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|A\psi\rangle + \langle\phi|B\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|\psi\rangle + \langle B\phi|\psi\rangle = \langle(A+B)\phi|\psi\rangle\). Similarly for scalar multiples. For \(A^2\): \(\langle\phi|A^2\psi\rangle = \langle A\phi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A^2\phi|\psi\rangle\).

□
Definition 5.5.

The Hamiltonian \(H = \frac{|\mathbf{P}|^2}{2m} + V(\mathbf{X})\) is the energy observable. Acting on wave functions: \((H\psi)(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi\). Refs: Definition 5.4, Lemma 5.1.

\[ H = \frac{|\mathbf{P}|^2}{2m} + V(\mathbf{X}) \] (5.11)
Definition 5.6.

An eigenstate of observable \(A\) satisfies \(A\psi = \alpha\psi\) for eigenvalue \(\alpha\). The set of all eigenvalues is the spectrum of \(A\). Ref: Definition 5.3.

\[ A\psi = \alpha\psi \] (5.16)
Proposition 5.2.

Eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators are real. Eigenstates with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal: \(\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = 0\) when \(\alpha_1 \neq \alpha_2\). Refs: Definition 5.3, Definition 5.6.

Proof.

\(\alpha\|\psi\|^2 = \langle\psi|A\psi\rangle = \langle A\psi|\psi\rangle = \bar{\alpha}\|\psi\|^2\), so \(\alpha = \bar{\alpha}\) is real. For orthogonality: \(\alpha_1\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = \langle A\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = \langle\psi_1|A\psi_2\rangle = \alpha_2\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\), so \((\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = 0\).

□
Theorem 5.3.

If \(A\) is self-adjoint on a finite-dimensional \(\mathcal{H}\), there exists a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. This generalises to infinite dimensions under suitable conditions: any \(\psi \in \mathcal{H}\) can be expanded as \(\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_n\) where \(A\psi_n = \alpha_n\psi_n\). Refs: Proposition 5.2, Definition 5.6.

5.3 Measurement

Definition 5.7.

Measuring observable \(A\) in state \(\psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_n\) yields eigenvalue \(\alpha_n\) with probability \(|c_n|^2\). Only eigenvalues can be observed as measurement outcomes. Refs: Theorem 5.3, Definition 5.6.

\[ P(\alpha_n) = |c_n|^2 = |\langle\psi_n|\psi\rangle|^2 \] (5.32)

5.4 Collapse of wave function

Definition 5.8.

After measuring \(A\) and obtaining eigenvalue \(\alpha_n\), the state instantaneously collapses to the eigenstate \(\psi_n\). All prior superposition information is lost. Ref: Definition 5.7.

5.5 Summary

The time evolution of a quantum state is governed by the Schrodinger equation in operator form:

\[ i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)} = H\ket{\psi(t)} \] (5.36)

6. Statistical aspects of quantum theory

Expectation values, commutators, and the uncertainty principle.

6.1 Expectation and dispersion

Definition 6.1.

The expectation value of observable \(A\) in normalized state \(\psi\) is: \[E_\psi(A) = \langle\psi|A\psi\rangle = \sum_n \alpha_n|c_n|^2.\] It equals the average over repeated measurements. Refs: Definition 5.7, Theorem 5.3.

\[ E_\psi(A) = \langle\psi|A\psi\rangle \] (6.1)
Definition 6.2.

The identity operator \(\mathbf{1}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{1}\psi = \psi\). An operator \(A\) is non-negative if \(\langle\psi|A\psi\rangle \geq 0\) for all \(\psi \in \mathcal{H}\).

Proposition 6.1.

Expectation is linear: \(E(A+B) = E(A) + E(B)\), \(E(\alpha A) = \alpha E(A)\). For non-negative \(A\), \(E(A) \geq 0\). Also \(E(\mathbf{1}) = 1\). Refs: Definition 6.1, Definition 6.2.

Definition 6.3.

The dispersion of observable \(A\) in state \(\psi\) is: \[\Delta_\psi(A) = \sqrt{E_\psi(A^2) - (E_\psi(A))^2}.\] It measures the spread of measurement outcomes. Ref: Definition 6.1.

\[ \Delta_\psi(A)^2 = E_\psi(A^2) - \bigl(E_\psi(A)\bigr)^2 \] (6.5)
Proposition 6.2.

\(\Delta_\psi(A) = 0\) if and only if \(\psi\) is an eigenstate of \(A\). In that case the eigenvalue equals \(E_\psi(A)\) and measurement is certain. Refs: Definition 6.3, Definition 5.6.

Proof.

\(\Delta_\psi(A)^2 = \|(A - E_\psi(A)\mathbf{1})\psi\|^2\) by self-adjointness. This is zero if and only if \(A\psi = E_\psi(A)\psi\).

□

6.2 Commutation relations

Definition 6.4.

The commutator of operators \(A\) and \(B\) is \([A,B] = AB - BA\). It measures non-commutativity of operators.

Proposition 6.3.

In 1D: \([X,P] = i\hbar\mathbf{1}\). In 3D: \([X_i,P_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}\mathbf{1}\), \([X_i,X_j] = 0 = [P_i,P_j]\). Refs: Definition 5.4, Definition 6.4.

Proof.

\((XP - PX)\psi = x(-i\hbar\psi') - (-i\hbar)(x\psi)' = -i\hbar x\psi' + i\hbar\psi + i\hbar x\psi' = i\hbar\psi\).

□
\[ [X, P] = i\hbar\,\mathbf{1} \] (6.9)
Proposition 6.4.

The commutator is: (i) antisymmetric: \([A,B] = -[B,A]\); (ii) linear; (iii) satisfies the Leibniz rule: \([A,BC] = B[A,C] + [A,B]C\); (iv) satisfies the Jacobi identity: \([A,[B,C]] + [B,[C,A]] + [C,[A,B]] = 0\); (v) if \([A,B] = iC\) with \(A,B\) self-adjoint, then \(C\) is self-adjoint. Ref: Definition 6.4.

6.3 Heisenberg uncertainty principle

Proposition 6.5.

For self-adjoint \(A, B\) with \([A,B] = iC\): \[E_\psi(A^2)E_\psi(B^2) \geq \frac{1}{4}(E_\psi(C))^2.\] Equality holds if and only if \((A - is_0 B)\psi = 0\) for some real \(s_0\). Refs: Definition 6.4, Definition 6.1.

Proof.

\(\|(A - isB)\psi\|^2 = E(A^2) + sE(C) + s^2E(B^2) \geq 0\) for all real \(s\). This quadratic in \(s\) has non-positive discriminant: \((E(C))^2 - 4E(A^2)E(B^2) \leq 0\).

□
\[ E_\psi(A^2)\,E_\psi(B^2) \geq \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(E_\psi(C)\bigr)^2 \] (6.13)
Corollary 6.6.

For normalized \(\psi\): \[\Delta_\psi(X)\Delta_\psi(P) \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.\] Equality holds for Gaussian wave functions \(\psi \propto e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\) (minimum uncertainty states). Refs: Proposition 6.5, Proposition 6.3.

Proof.

Set \(A = X - E(X)\mathbf{1}\), \(B = P - E(P)\mathbf{1}\). Then \([A,B] = [X,P] = i\hbar\mathbf{1}\), so \(C = \hbar\mathbf{1}\). Apply Proposition 6.5 with \(E(A^2) = \Delta(X)^2\) and \(E(B^2) = \Delta(P)^2\).

□
\[ \Delta_\psi(X)\,\Delta_\psi(P) \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} \] (6.15)
Corollary 6.7.

For any self-adjoint \(A, B\): \[\Delta_\psi(A)\Delta_\psi(B) \geq \frac{1}{2}|E_\psi([A,B])|.\] Non-commuting observables cannot both be measured with arbitrary precision. Refs: Proposition 6.5, Definition 6.4.

7. The harmonic oscillator revisited

Algebraic approach using raising and lowering operators. The Hamiltonian is \(H = \frac{P^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 X^2\).

7.1 Raising and lowering operators

Definition 7.1.

The raising and lowering operators are: \[a_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}(\mp iP + m\omega X).\] Since \(X\) and \(P\) are self-adjoint, \(a_+ = (a_-)^*\). Ref: Definition 5.4.

\[ a_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}\bigl(\mp iP + m\omega X\bigr) \] (7.1)
Lemma 7.1.

The Hamiltonian factorizes as \(H = (N + \frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega\), where \(N = a_+a_-\) is the self-adjoint number operator. Refs: Definition 7.1, Proposition 6.3.

Proof.

\(N = a_+a_- = \frac{1}{2m\omega\hbar}(P^2 + m^2\omega^2 X^2 + im\omega[X,P]) = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\!\left(\frac{P^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 X^2\right) - \frac{1}{2}\). Rearranging gives the result.

□
\[ H = \left(N + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad N = a_+a_- \] (7.5)
Definition 7.2.

The number operator \(N = a_+a_-\) counts the excitation level. Its eigenvalue on \(\psi_n\) is \(n\), giving energy \(E_n = (n + \frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega\). Refs: Definition 7.1, Lemma 7.1.

Proposition 7.2.

(i) \([a_-, a_+] = 1\). (ii) \([N, a_\pm] = \pm a_\pm\). (iii) \(\|a_-\psi\|^2 = \langle\psi|N\psi\rangle\). (iv) \(\|a_+\psi\|^2 = \langle\psi|(N+1)\psi\rangle\). Refs: Definition 7.1, Definition 7.2, Proposition 6.3.

\[ N(a_\pm\psi) = (\lambda \pm 1)\,a_\pm\psi \quad\text{if } N\psi = \lambda\psi \] (7.12)
Lemma 7.3.

If \(N\psi = \lambda\psi\) with \(\psi \neq 0\): (a) \(a_\pm\psi\) are eigenstates of \(N\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda \pm 1\) (if non-zero); (b) \(\lambda \geq 0\) with equality if and only if \(a_-\psi = 0\). Refs: Definition 7.2, Proposition 7.2.

Proof.

(a) \(N(a_\pm\psi) = ([N,a_\pm] + a_\pm N)\psi = (\pm a_\pm + \lambda a_\pm)\psi = (\lambda\pm 1)a_\pm\psi\). (b) \(\lambda = \langle\psi|N\psi\rangle/\|\psi\|^2 = \|a_-\psi\|^2/\|\psi\|^2 \geq 0\), with equality if and only if \(a_-\psi = 0\).

□
Theorem 7.4.

The spectrum of \(N\) is \(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\). If the ground state \(\psi_0\) is unique, the full set of eigenstates is \(\{a_+^n\psi_0 : n \geq 0\}\), each with eigenvalue \(n\). Refs: Lemma 7.3, Proposition 7.2.

Proof.

If \(N\psi = \mu\psi\), applying \(a_-\) repeatedly gives eigenstates with eigenvalues \(\mu, \mu-1, \ldots\). Since eigenvalues \(\geq 0\), this terminates at some \(a_-^n\psi = 0\) with \(\mu - n = 0\). Hence \(\mu \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\). Conversely, \(a_+^n\psi_0\) has eigenvalue \(n\).

□

7.2 Normalized states

Proposition 7.6.

The normalized stationary states are: \[\psi_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}a_+^n\psi_0,\] forming an orthonormal set \(\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}\). Refs: Theorem 7.4, Proposition 5.2.

8. Angular momentum

In classical mechanics, angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{x} \wedge \mathbf{p}\). Conservation of angular momentum plays a key role in the hydrogen atom.

8.1 Angular momentum operators

Definition 8.1.

The angular momentum operator \(\mathbf{L} = (L_1, L_2, L_3)\) has components: \[L_i = \sum_{j,k} \epsilon_{ijk} X_j P_k,\] where \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is the Levi-Civita alternating symbol. See Definition 5.4.

Proposition 8.1.

The angular momentum components satisfy the following commutation relations:

(i) \([L_i, X_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} X_k\).

(ii) \([L_i, P_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} P_k\).

(iii) \([L_i, L_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} L_k\).

The last relation shows that angular momentum components do not commute. See Definition 8.1, Proposition 6.3, Proposition 6.4.

Proof.

For (i): \([L_i, X_j] = \sum_{m,n}\epsilon_{imn}[X_mP_n, X_j] = \sum_{m,n}\epsilon_{imn}X_m[P_n,X_j] = -i\hbar\sum_m \epsilon_{imj}X_m = i\hbar\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}X_k\). Parts (ii) and (iii) are similar, using the Leibniz rule and the canonical commutation relations.

□
\[ [L_i, L_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk}\,L_k \] (8.2)

8.2 Raising and lowering operators

Definition 8.2.

A general angular momentum operator is any self-adjoint vector operator \(\mathbf{J} = (J_1, J_2, J_3)\) satisfying: \[[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar \sum_k \epsilon_{ijk} J_k.\] This includes orbital angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\) and spin. See Proposition 8.1.

Definition 8.3.

The total angular momentum squared operator is \(J^2 = J_1^2 + J_2^2 + J_3^2\). It measures the total angular momentum magnitude, and is self-adjoint and non-negative. See Definition 8.2, Lemma 5.1.

Proposition 8.2.

\([J^2, J_i] = 0\) for all \(i\). Hence \(J^2\) and any single component \(J_i\) can be simultaneously measured -- they share a common set of eigenstates. See Definition 8.3, Definition 8.2.

Proof.

Using the Leibniz rule and Definition 8.2: \[[J^2, J_3] = [J_1^2, J_3] + [J_2^2, J_3] = J_1[J_1,J_3] + [J_1,J_3]J_1 + J_2[J_2,J_3] + [J_2,J_3]J_2\] \[= -i\hbar(J_1J_2 + J_2J_1) + i\hbar(J_2J_1 + J_1J_2) = 0.\] The cases \(i = 1, 2\) are analogous by symmetry.

□

8.3 Representations of angular momentum

Theorem 8.3.

The simultaneous eigenvalues of \(J^2\) and \(J_3\) are \(j(j+1)\hbar^2\) and \(m\hbar\), where \(j \in \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\) and \(m \in \{-j, -j+1, \ldots, j\}\). For orbital angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\), only integer values \(j = l \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\) occur. See Proposition 8.2, Definition 8.2.

Proof.

Define raising and lowering operators \(J_\pm = J_1 \pm iJ_2\). One verifies \([J_3, J_\pm] = \pm\hbar J_\pm\) and \([J_+, J_-] = 2\hbar J_3\). If \(\psi\) is an eigenstate of \(J_3\) with eigenvalue \(m\hbar\), then \(J_\pm\psi\) (when nonzero) is an eigenstate with eigenvalue \((m\pm 1)\hbar\). By the same ladder argument as for the harmonic oscillator, the spectrum of \(J_3\) is bounded above and below; the ladder must terminate, which forces \(m\) to range from \(-j\) to \(j\) in integer steps for some \(j \in \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\). Using \(J^2 = J_-J_+ + J_3^2 + \hbar J_3\) gives the eigenvalue \(j(j+1)\hbar^2\).

□
\[ J^2\ket{j,m} = j(j+1)\hbar^2\ket{j,m}, \qquad J_3\ket{j,m} = m\hbar\ket{j,m} \] (8.11)

8.4 Orbital angular momentum and spherical harmonics

Definition 8.4.

The spherical harmonics \(Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)\) are the simultaneous eigenstates of \(L^2\) and \(L_3\) for orbital angular momentum: \[L^2 Y_l^m = l(l+1)\hbar^2 Y_l^m, \qquad L_3 Y_l^m = m\hbar Y_l^m,\] where \(l \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\) and \(m \in \{-l, \ldots, l\}\). They form a complete orthonormal set on the sphere \(S^2\). See Theorem 8.3, Definition 8.1.

\[ L^2 Y_l^m = l(l+1)\hbar^2 Y_l^m, \qquad L_3 Y_l^m = m\hbar Y_l^m \] (8.33)

9. The hydrogen atom

The hydrogen atom consists of an electron orbiting a proton under the Coulomb potential \(V(r) = -e^2/r\).

9.1 Atoms

An atom consists of a heavy nucleus of charge \(Ze\) surrounded by electrons. For hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)), we consider a single electron in the Coulomb field of the proton.

9.2 Central potentials

Definition 9.1.

A central potential is a potential \(V = V(r)\) depending only on the distance \(r = |\mathbf{x}|\). The stationary Schrodinger equation separates in spherical coordinates: the angular part is solved by spherical harmonics \(Y_l^m\), and the radial part becomes: \[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}u\right) + V(r)u = Eu,\] where \(\psi(r,\theta,\phi) = \frac{u(r)}{r}Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)\).

\[ H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 - \frac{e^2}{r} \] (9.10)

9.3 Spectrum of hydrogen atom

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left(-\frac{e^2}{r} + \frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2m_e r^2}\right)u = E\,u \] (9.14)
Theorem 9.1.

For the Coulomb potential \(V(r) = -e^2/r\), the bound-state energy levels of the hydrogen atom are: \[E_n = -\frac{m_e e^4}{2\hbar^2 n^2}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}.\] Each energy level \(E_n\) has degeneracy \(n^2\), with \(l = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1\) and \(m = -l, \ldots, l\). This reproduces the Rydberg formula \(\omega_{n_1,n_2} = 2\pi R_0 c\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)\). See Definition 9.1, Definition 8.4, Definition 1.1.

\[ E_n = -\frac{m_e e^4}{2\hbar^2 n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \] (9.32)

9.4 Rotationally symmetric solutions

For \(l = 0\) (s-wave states), the angular part is trivial (\(Y_0^0 = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}\)) and the wave function depends only on \(r\).

10. Quantum key distribution

Quantum mechanics has applications beyond physics. The BB84 protocol, developed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984, uses the quantum measurement postulate and the no-cloning theorem to establish secure communication channels. Any eavesdropping attempt disturbs the quantum states being transmitted, making interception detectable. This is a direct consequence of the collapse of the wave function: measuring a quantum state inevitably changes it.

The security of quantum key distribution rests on fundamental quantum mechanical principles rather than computational assumptions, unlike classical cryptographic methods such as RSA. The uncertainty principle ensures that an eavesdropper cannot simultaneously determine all properties of the transmitted quantum states.
